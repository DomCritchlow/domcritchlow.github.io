Calendars Are Kind of Boring

November 05, 2026 · Design

What happens when the calendar itself becomes the visual surface for a conversation?

A calendar is a good picture of time. You can tell which mornings are crowded, where the gaps are, and how much of a week has already been spoken for.

Then you try to change it.

You drag a meeting. Another one is in the way. You open a form, choose a different time, go back to the week and check what happened. Or you tell an assistant what you want and receive a tidy paragraph describing the result. Either way, the thinking happens somewhere other than the picture.

What if the calendar could respond as part of the conversation?

This is a set of small interactive studies drawn from the design and interaction work behind Dayweave. They use made-up meetings and scripted behavior. Nothing connects to a real calendar.

1. Give attention some room #

Most calendar layouts divide the available space evenly. Every day gets the same width, even when only two days matter to the question.

Select Thursday. It gets more room, but the other days stay in place. Then select Tuesday and Thursday together.

FOCUS AND CONTEXTThe whole week
Select a day to expand it. Select another to compare. Select both again to return to the week.

This is the fisheye idea: keep the context, but give the part you're working on more space. It isn't new. DateLens, the original CHI 2003 fisheye-calendar paper, explored precisely this tension between detail and overview.

The interesting next step is to let the question determine the focus, rather than forcing the person to do all the navigation.

2. The calendar can listen before you press Enter #

Imagine typing “find two hours Thursday afternoon.” There are three useful pieces of information in that sentence: a day, part of the day, and duration. None requires a model to guess what you mean.

Start typing the request, or use the example. Watch Thursday widen and the possible windows appear while the sentence is still a draft.

Dayweave's implementation uses a local parser for this first response. It can recognize named dates, parts of days, durations and meetings. The view reacts immediately, while the real interpretation and reasoning happen after the request is submitted.

A draft is a lens, not an instruction to move anything.

3. One sentence can move several things #

Now consider a week that is already full.

“Give me two hours to work on the prototype, group the catch-ups together, and keep some space before Friday's review.”

A text answer could list five changes. But it's much easier to judge the consequences when the changes remain on the calendar.

Show the proposal. The highlighted events move to their suggested positions. Other commitments remain where they were.

This is where the calendar is doing more than displaying a result. Moving three meetings to make room for one block is a trade-off. You can see the cost at a glance. You might decide the proposal solves the wrong problem.

4. A correction should change the picture, not restart the chat #

A proposal is not a booking. Saying “actually, Thursday needs to stay free” should revise the plan, not require a new conversation about a different week.

Add the constraint below. Notice what changes, and what doesn't.

A CONVERSATION WITH CONSEQUENCESOne possible plan
YouMake time for deep work without breaking up my meetings.
CalendarOne option is to use Thursday afternoon.
WEDNESDAYTeam sync · 2 pm

FRIDAYProject review · 11 am
A preview only. No meetings changed.

This sounds small, but it changes what a conversation means. You're no longer asking a system to do something out of sight. You're making and revising a visual plan.

The interface needs to distinguish three separate states: what is in focus, what is being proposed, and what is actually booked. A possible move can be drawn on the grid without claiming it has happened. An organizer request can be shown as waiting for agreement. Unknown availability must stay unknown.

That distinction matters much more than making events animate beautifully.

A different kind of calendar #

The conventional grid is still a useful starting point. The change is in what the grid can do.

It can expand around a question. It can acknowledge a sentence before the sentence is finished. It can display several changes together, with their consequences. And when the answer isn't quite right, it can keep the relevant context on screen while the plan changes again.

Maybe the calendar doesn't need more chat. It needs to become the thing we're talking through.
