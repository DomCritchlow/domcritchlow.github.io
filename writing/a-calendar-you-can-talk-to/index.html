Show me, don’t tell me

Dominic Critchlow  ·  November 22, 2026

I’ll gladly ask an LLM to go through twenty meetings and tell me which ones I can skip. Doing that myself means opening every invite. But if I’m going to move something, I want my calendar in front of me. I’m a visual learner. I want to point at Friday, try Thursday, and see what I’ve done to both days. I built this because getting that view usually takes me more clicks than moving the meeting.

I might ask this out loud. I might just click the Friday hold. Either way, once I see it sitting right up against Thursday lunch, I have a better question: do I want Friday morning free badly enough to give up that break?

Give the answer room

Ask which meetings you could skip. Three come forward. Each carries its own reason. The vendor demo will be recorded. Roadmap office hours are a drop-in. Notes from all hands come out Monday. You can weigh those reasons against the rest of your week while you read them.

Design note · How much can the week shrink?

The hard part was deciding how much the rest of the week should give way. My first pass squeezed some hours down to 26 pixels and cut off their text. I could read everything about the meeting I’d picked and too little about the one right after it. The version above keeps surrounding hours at least 84% of their usual height.

Open the detail in place

The same principle applies inside a meeting. I may only need to know who’s coming. If I need the agenda too, the meeting can grow another line or two in the place I already found it.

Let the question choose the view

Suppose I’m choosing between this Tuesday and next Thursday. A week view makes me flip back and forth, remembering the first day while I inspect the second. Put them next to each other and I can compare them directly. The narrow strip between them matters. It tells me how much time I’ve folded away.

Let the screen finish the question

Now run that idea backwards. Once I’ve put two days on screen, I should be able to ask a question about “these” and have the system use the view I just made. Choosing what to look at is already part of telling the computer what I mean.

Draw the change while it’s still a choice

Move Monday’s project review to Wednesday at eleven. For a moment I need to see that meeting twice. Monday tells me what I’d free up. Wednesday tells me what I’d be putting next to it. An outline can hold the old time while I inspect the new one.

The final button needs a precise verb. Moving a meeting updates the guests. Declining an invitation tells its organizer I won’t attend. Canceling my own meeting takes it off everyone’s calendar. In early tests the model sometimes turned a decline into a cancellation. That distinction belongs in the application’s rules as well as on the button.

A suggestion can start somewhere else

A teammate flags a packed Friday in a conversation. An assistant reading that thread suggests moving my weekly reflection. It has a reason to suggest Thursday. The calendar has the information to check whether Thursday is still free. I want to see both before I decide.

Beyond the calendar

Fisheye calendars go back at least to DateLens in 2003. Selecting, comparing, and previewing changes are familiar interactions too. What used to be hard was knowing when to use each one. “Could I skip this?” calls for reasons attached to meetings. “What if we moved it?” calls for a picture of two possible Wednesdays. The same calendar can answer both if it has a way to draw the difference.

I’d ask the same of a spreadsheet. If I ask why a forecast fell, take me to the months that changed and the assumption they share. If I ask what happens at the old renewal rate, let me try it in those cells and see the total move. That’s what I mean by show me. I want to be able to point at the answer and ask the next question there.
